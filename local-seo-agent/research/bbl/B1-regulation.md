## 2. Scotland first: independent healthcare regulation (surgical and medical-injectable services)

### 2.1 HIS registration (not CQC)
- In Scotland, independent healthcare is regulated by Healthcare Improvement Scotland (HIS) under the National Health Service (Scotland) Act 1978. Providing an unregistered independent healthcare service is an offence (s.10Z9 per HIS-related snippet). Confidence: med-high. Source: https://www.healthcareimprovementscotland.scot/inspections-reviews-and-regulation/regulation-of-independent-healthcare/
- HIS regulates independent hospitals, independent clinics and independent medical agencies. An "independent clinic" is a clinic not in a hospital where a registered doctor, dental practitioner, registered nurse, midwife, pharmacist/pharmacy technician or dental care professional provides services (not NHS). Independent clinics were brought into regulation by SSIs from 1 April 2016. Cosmetic procedures are explicitly within scope when delivered by these professionals. Confidence: high. Source: https://www.healthcareimprovementscotland.scot/inspections-reviews-and-regulation/regulation-of-independent-healthcare/independent-healthcare-find-out-if-you-need-to-register/information-for-service-providers-independent-clinics/
- "Independent hospital" = a hospital that is neither an NHS hospital nor a private psychiatric hospital (1978 Act definition). Surgical BBL (general anaesthesia, overnight/day-surgery) belongs in an HIS-registered independent hospital; the exact boundary of what an "independent clinic" may do surgically was NOT verified [LAWYER/HIS].
- Differences from CQC (England): (a) different regulator and Act; (b) HIS grades inspection reports (reports use a quality framework with grades; examples seen include "Good"; the full scale was not confirmed from the methodology PDF - low confidence on exact labels); (c) England's regulation 20A (display of CQC rating on premises and website, within 21 days of publication) has NO verified Scottish equivalent. (d) HIS conditions: services are expected to display their HIS registration certificate on premises; I found no verified statutory duty to display HIS grades or a registration number on a website. Confidence: med (absence of evidence, not evidence of absence). HIS publishes a public register and inspection reports, so the agent can verify registration externally: https://www.healthcareimprovementscotland.scot/
- PHIN does display HIS regulator ratings for hospitals (https://www.phin.org.uk/help/About-Healthcare-Improvement-Scotland-Regulator-Ratings) - evidence PHIN covers Scottish private hospitals. [LAWYER] to confirm whether CMA Order duties apply to a Scottish cosmetic-only provider (the CMA Order is UK-wide for private healthcare; I could not confirm cosmetic-surgery scope).

### 2.2 Scottish Government position: non-surgical procedures
- Act: Non-surgical Procedures and Functions of Medical Reviewers (Scotland) Act 2026 (asp 2026/13). Passed by Parliament March 2026 (sources say 17 or 18 March), Royal Assent 12 May 2026. Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2026/13 ; https://www.parliament.scot/bills-and-laws/bills/s6/non-surgical-procedures-and-functions-of-medical-reviewers-scotland-bill . Confidence: med-high.
- Content (secondary summaries): list of regulated procedures in Schedule 1 includes injectable procedures (filler, "including non-surgical BBL"), thread lifts, IV procedures, ablative laser, deeper chemical peels, microneedling 1.5mm+; offence to provide to under-18s or outside "permitted premises" (HIS-registered independent hospital/clinic with a doctor/dentist/nurse/midwife/pharmacist prescriber; NHS GP/dental practices; registered pharmacies); fine up to GBP 20,000; enforcement powers for HIS. Commencement: not before September 2027. Confidence: med. The Act does not list body parts; liquid BBL is covered via "injectable" definition (inference from secondary sources - [LAWYER]).
- Separate Order: Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982 (Licensing of Non-surgical Procedures) Order 2026 (SSI 2026/162) - licensable activity from 6 September 2027 for lower-risk procedures (non-ablative laser/light incl. IPL/LED, superficial peels, cryolipolysis, cryotherapy, electrocautery, HIFU, radiofrequency, microneedling <1.5mm). Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/sdsi/2026/9780111064603 ; explainer https://spice-spotlight.scot/2026/01/14/scotlands-new-licensing-rules-what-cosmetic-procedures-need-a-licence-and-how-will-it-work/ . Confidence: med. NOT verified: whether ultrasonic "cavitation" (low-frequency) or vacuum/massage fall within the list. Snippets say the licensing scope is procedures that "pierce or penetrate the skin", which would exclude much surface-device work - conflicting with RF/HIFU being listed. Treat as UNRESOLVED [LAWYER]/ask North Lanarkshire licensing.
- Surgical cosmetic procedures in Scotland are already within HIS independent-hospital regulation; I found no Scottish bill specific to surgical cosmetic procedures 2024-2026 (low confidence - negative search result).
- Local enforcement precedent: Glasgow City Council Environmental Health served Health and Safety prohibition notices (2024) on businesses/an individual barring BBL and breast-augmentation procedures within the city after a hotel-function-room BBL led to emergency admission. Source: https://www.glasgow.gov.uk/article/9206/Firms-Banned-from-Carrying-Out-Brazilian-Butt-Lifts-in-Glasgow . Confidence: high that it happened; shows councils can act on BBL providers even without a licensing regime.

### 2.3 Who may perform (law vs best practice) - UK-wide for doctors
- Legally, a fully registered, licensed GMC doctor may perform any procedure they consider themselves competent to perform; specialist-register (plastic surgery) status is NOT a legal requirement for private cosmetic surgery. Best practice: RCS England cosmetic certification (only available to those on the GMC specialist register), following the 2013 Keogh Review. Confidence: med-high. Sources: https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/news-and-events/blog/make-surgery-safer/ ; Keogh: http://data.parliament.uk/DepositedPapers/Files/DEP2013-0717/Review_of_the_Regulation_of_Cosmetic_Interventions.pdf
- Consequence for the crawler: absence of "specialist register" is not a legal breach, but claims such as "consultant plastic surgeon" / "board-certified" must match the GMC register (see Section 12). "Surgeon" title use by non-surgical practitioners is a known issue (JPRAS-type literature snippet).

### 2.4 Consent and Scots-law specifics
- Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board [2015] UKSC 11 (a Scottish appeal; Supreme Court, UK-wide effect): doctors must ensure patients know of "material risks" and reasonable alternatives (reasonable-patient test). Applies directly to BBL consent and should shape risk-information requirements. Confidence: high. Source: https://ukscblog.com/case-comment-montgomery-v-lanarkshire-health-board-2015-uksc-11/
- Scotland-specific cooling-off/advertising law for cosmetic procedures: none found beyond UK-wide ASA/CAP and GMC. The consent age in Scotland (16+ for surgical treatment under the Age of Legal Capacity (Scotland) Act 1991) differs from England in principle; the 2026 Act's under-18 ban applies to non-surgical procedures only. [LAWYER] - not verified by search.
- NHS Scotland vs private: HIS also inspects NHS services under a different regime; private patients are outside NHS Scotland funding and complaints routes (HIS handles independent-clinic complaints/enforcement; NHS complaints go to the board/SPSO). Low confidence on complaint-route details.

## 3. NON-SURGICAL body contouring ("booty lift" devices) in Scotland - separate section

### 3.1 Is it HIS-regulated?
- HIS registration is triggered by who provides the service and what it is (doctor, nurse, midwife, pharmacist, dental professional delivering treatment). A purely beauty-therapist-run, non-medical device service is, on the HIS definitions found, outside HIS registration. Confidence: med. The point to check per site: does a nurse/doctor deliver or supervise any part, or are injectables/"prescription" products used? If yes, HIS registration is likely required [LAWYER/HIS]. The brand's pages (not fetched - blocked) should be crawled for "nurse", "doctor", "prescriber", "injectable".
- From 6 Sept 2027, RF-type and HIFU/cryolipolysis device treatments become licensable by the council; cavitation/vacuum status unresolved (Section 2.2). The agent should WARN (not state as fact) that a licence may be required from that date and recommend checking with North Lanarkshire Council Licensing.

### 3.2 Local authority licensing (Scotland)
- Councils license skin piercing/tattooing and, in some areas, other activities under the Civic Government (Scotland) Act 1982. North Lanarkshire Council runs a skin piercing and tattooing licence with national conditions: https://www.northlanarkshire.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2025-06/Skin%20Piercing%20%20Tattooing%20Application%20Form.pdf . I did NOT find a verified North Lanarkshire "massage and special treatment" licence for beauty-device salons (low confidence; some Scottish councils have such licences - [LAWYER]/council to confirm). Premises also face building warrant/Environmental Health, health and safety, and (if cosmetic lasers/IPL) laser safety expectations.

### 3.3 Insurance and qualification expectations
- No statutory qualification requirement for non-medical device operators exists in Scotland today (the 2026 regime defers to future regulations for qualifications - Section 5 of the Act gives Scottish Ministers powers; voluntary guidance referenced in a gov.scot FOI page). Treat insurance/qualifications as WARN/REQUIRE-DISCLOSURE rather than legal checks: practitioner insurance covering the specific device treatment, manufacturer training certificates, patch-test/contraindication screening. Confidence: low-med.
- Contraindications for RF/cavitation (pregnancy, pacemakers/metal implants, DVT/clotting disorders, filler or implants in area, kidney/liver disease, cancer) are standard in manufacturers' guidance and industry literature; the site must carry them. [CLINICIAN] for the definitive list.

### 3.4 ASA/CAP for beauty/health device claims (UK-wide, applies now)
- Objective efficacy claims need evidence "if relevant consisting of trials conducted on people" (CAP Code 12.1; substantiation guidance https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation-for-health-beauty-and-slimming-claims.html ). Confidence: high.
- Cellulite: ASA/CAP have not seen convincing evidence that devices/cosmetic treatments treat, remove or reduce cellulite or its appearance (any wording: "orange peel", "dimpled skin"). "Erase cellulite" (client's own listing) is therefore a REFUSE-grade claim without product-specific rigorous evidence. Confidence: high. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/weight-control-cellulite.html
- Slimming/spot reduction: CAP Code section 13: weight-reduction effectiveness claims need rigorous human trials; claims that weight/fat can be lost from specific body parts are not accepted (one snippet cites rule 13.9 - med). Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/13.html
- "Fat melting/fat destroyer" device claims: an ASA 2023 ruling on a hand-held device ("fat annihilator", "fat destroyer", "dissolve cellulite and wee it out") found the ads misleading for lack of evidence (advertiser name not captured in my results; low-med confidence on date). Earlier: Slimspired / Medspa Pro Ltd (2017) and The Slimline Clinic (2012) rulings against unsubstantiated ultrasound fat/inch-loss claims (secondary snippet; med). I did NOT find an ASA ruling specifically about vacuum/cavitation "butt lift" devices (checked; none surfaced).
- "Safest", "permanent", "tighten loose skin": absolute/superlative safety claims need evidence and trivialise risk (the 2025 liquid-BBL rulings treat "safe" as problematic); "permanent" claims for skin tightening/fat reduction by non-invasive devices are unlikely to be substantiated. Confidence: med-high.
- Before/after images: need signed, dated documentary proof of genuineness; filters/retouching must not exaggerate; retain unretouched originals; images alone do not substantiate efficacy. Source: ASA/CAP help note (https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-and-resources/cap-bitesize/rules-for-advertising-botox.html is the Botox page; the before/after guidance is summarised at https://www.mishcon.com/news/advertising-before-and-after-pictures-key-takeaways-from-caps-latest-guidance ). Confidence: high on content; I could not open the primary.
- Testimonials: hold documentary evidence they are genuine and contact details of the giver; claims within them must not mislead (CAP 3.45-3.49 cluster; one snippet lists 3.45, another 3.47 for the same duty - verify numbering). Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html
- Under-18s: CAP 12.25 applies to "cosmetic interventions" = any intervention with the primary objective of changing physical appearance, surgical or non-surgical, invasive or non-invasive. A non-invasive body-contouring service is arguably within the definition; treat as applicable. Confidence: med (definition is verbatim; application to device treatments is my inference). Also the 2026 Scottish Act bans under-18 non-surgical procedures for the listed categories.
- Time-limited/pressure offers and "trivialising" apply to the non-surgical service too under 1.3 (the 12.25 guidance covers non-surgical interventions), though the 2025 rulings concerned liquid BBL. Confidence: med.

### 3.5 Does calling a non-surgical treatment a "Brazilian butt lift" mislead?
- No ASA ruling or guidance found specifically on this question (low confidence; negative search). Reasoning from principles (CAP 3.1 misleading, 3.7 substantiation): "Brazilian butt lift" is a recognised term for fat-transfer surgery; "liquid/non-surgical BBL" is a recognised (and high-risk) filler procedure. A device-only service named "Brazilian Booty Lift" (a brand name, "BBLL" abbreviation) implies a comparable lifting/volumising outcome. The agent should WARN that: (i) the name implies volume augmentation that skin-surface devices are unlikely to match; (ii) any copy must state clearly "non-surgical, no filler, no fat transfer" and avoid comparison claims ("alternative to surgery", "same results as BBL") unless substantiated; (iii) consumers searching "BBL" may reach the page - set honest expectations. [LAWYER] for trademark/branding. Also note the brand claims "revolution of the non-invasive Brazilian Booty Lift" - the word "revolution"/"breakthrough" are novelty claims requiring evidence.
- "Lift" claims: lifting/tightening results from non-invasive devices need human-trial evidence specific to the device and the buttock area.

### 3.6 Franchise marketing responsibility
- CAP Code applies to marketing communications by the marketer; each local franchisee that publishes ads is the advertiser for its local page/social posts, and the franchisor is the marketer for brand site/central creative. The ASA looks at who is responsible for the content and may name either/both (principle from ASA's approach to influencers/affiliates - the brand is typically held responsible; general inference, med-low). Practical rules: (1) the local page must not inherit non-compliant brand claims - the agent should audit the local page AND the brand page it links to; (2) franchise-wide offers (percentage-off, time-limited) are the franchisor's but run by locals - flag both; (3) review/testimonial duties under DMCC apply to whoever publishes the reviews. [LAWYER] for contract allocation and indemnities.

## 4. England context (brief, for non-Scottish clients)
- CQC: cosmetic surgery is a regulated activity ("surgical procedures"); unregistered provision is an offence. Regulation 20A of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014: rated providers must display the rating conspicuously on premises and on their website, and update within 21 days of publication. Source: https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-20a . Confidence: high. Wales (HIW), NI (RQIA): not researched here.

## 5. GMC guidance for doctors who offer cosmetic interventions (UK-wide, applies to Scottish doctors)
Source: https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/cosmetic-interventions (in effect 1 June 2016). Confidence on content: high; on paragraph numbers: med (from search-result snippets).
- Para 16: the doctor performing the intervention must discuss it and seek consent; must not delegate. Verbatim seen.
- Paras 17-22: ask why the patient wants it; if unlikely to benefit, must not provide it; consider vulnerabilities/psychological needs; explain follow-up at the outset; disclose lower-risk alternatives; give clear, accurate risk information (incl. anaesthesia/sedation). Verbatim seen.
- Paras 24-27: time for reflection ("cooling off"). A 2-week minimum is widely reported (Keogh/RCS, and secondary articles attributing it to the GMC); I could not confirm the GMC text sets a fixed period - treat as "reflection period required; 2 weeks is the commonly cited standard for surgery" (med-low).
- Paras ~47-53: marketing must be responsible, follow ASA/CAP; must not minimise or trivialise risks or exploit vulnerability; must not use promotional tactics that might tempt ill-considered decisions (e.g. financial inducements, time-limited or two-for-one offers, discounts); para 53: must not offer services as a prize. Confidence: high on substance, med on numbers. An MPS/GMC learning page "Laser eye surgery and time-limited offers" exists as GMC learning material (https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/learning-materials/laser-eye-surgery-and-time-limited-offers).
- Before/after photos, testimonials, social media, cost information: GMC expects costs to be given clearly before consent; photos should be genuine, same conditions, dated, not manipulated (one summary); testimonials: GMC social-media and advertising guidance plus ASA (Section 3.4). I did not see explicit GMC text on soliciting patient reviews - gap.
- Review replies: GMC confidentiality guidance ("responding to criticism in the media") says doctors should usually limit public replies to an explanation of their duty of confidentiality - do not confirm the person was a patient. Confidence: high. Source: https://www.gponline.com/responding-online-complaints-patients/article/1456425 (secondary).
- Applies only to GMC-registered doctors; a non-medical beauty operator is not bound by GMC but CAP/ASA and DMCC apply.

## 8. Patient data and consent in marketing (UK-wide)

## 12. ENCODABLE AGENT BEHAVIOUR

Design principle: the agent drafts and audits copy; it is never a legal sign-off. Every output ends with a standard "not legal/medical advice; [LAWYER]/[CLINICIAN] items" line. Rules are tiered: REFUSE (do not produce; explain), WARN (produce only with caveat/fix), REQUIRE (must be present/ask client for evidence before publishing), CHECK (crawler or lookup test).

### 12.1 REFUSE (do not generate or optimise copy that does this)
R1. Any time-limited/pressure offer, countdown, Black Friday/seasonal discount, "last slots", multi-buy, 2-for-1, prize/giveaway of treatment, price-rise deadline for cosmetic surgery or injectables/liquid BBL (CAP 1.3 cases; GMC paras 52-53). For non-surgical beauty devices: WARN rather than REFUSE (consumer-law rules still apply; ASA 1.3 logic applies but is documented mainly for surgery/injectables) - confidence med.
R2. Absolute safety/pain/downtime claims for surgical or injectable BBL: "safe", "0% infection", "risk-free", "no downtime", "minimal pain", "painless", "sterile clinic". For non-surgical devices: REFUSE "risk-free/guaranteed/painless/no side effects"; WARN "safest/safe" and require evidence.
R3. Guaranteed outcomes; "permanent" results; "instant results"; "transform your body effortlessly"; exploiting insecurity ("boost your confidence", "perfect body").
R4. Cellulite removal/reduction claims and spot-reduction/inch-loss/fat-melting claims for devices without client-supplied human-trial evidence (ASA cellulite advice; CAP section 13).
R5. Targeting under-18s (media/context: school/teen interests, youth influencers, "prom"); producing under-18 appeals.
R6. Claiming or implying HIS/CQC/GMC registration, ratings, "approved by NHS/CQC/HIS", "clinically/medically proven", "doctor-led/nurse-led" when not verified.
R7. Fake/incentivised-without-disclosure reviews, review gating, review-for-discount, staff/family reviews (DMCC Sched 20 para 13; Google/Trustpilot policies). Also AI-written reviews.
R8. Using patient before/after images without documented explicit consent; filtered/retouched/non-comparable images; stock "before/after" images presented as the client's.
R9. Comparing a non-surgical device service to surgical/filler BBL results ("same as BBL without surgery", "alternative to BBL") unless client supplies substantiation [LAWYER]; claiming it "lifts/augments" volume.
R10. Promoting overseas "package"/"holiday" surgery or influencer #ad-free promotion; paid promotion to a procedure's targeted minors.
R11. Writing "review replies" that reveal patient identity/treatment.

### 12.2 WARN (allow with fix; show rule + safer wording)
W1. Finance: monthly-price-led headline ("from GBP x/month"), "0% finance", BNPL logos: lead with total price, representative examples, no "affordable to anyone" framing; third-party BNPL now FCA-regulated (from 15 Jul 2026) [LAWYER].
W2. Before/after: require signed dated consent + unretouched originals on file; same lighting/angle/pose; state interval/number of sessions; add "individual results vary" (necessary but does not cure misleading images).
W3. Influencer/affiliate content: "#ad" at the start of caption; client liable.
W4. Price statements: show total cost incl. consultation, aftercare, revision/complication policy; no "from" prices that hide real totals (CAP prices rules, CMA).
W5. Name/brand: "Brazilian butt/booty lift" for a non-surgical service - add explicit "non-surgical, no injections, no fat transfer" near the first mention; avoid keyword stuffing of "BBL" that targets surgical/filler searchers (SEO risk: misleading).
W6. Superlatives ("best", "number 1", "revolution", "breakthrough", "latest technology") need evidence.
W7. Content that gives medical advice without clinician review; Scottish/English law differences.
W8. Upcoming licensing: 6 Sept 2027 licensing (RF/HIFU/cryolipolysis etc.) and HIS "permitted premises" rules - advise client to confirm with council/HIS; do not state exact applicability.

### 12.3 REQUIRE (before publishing client content)
Q1. Evidence file: for each efficacy claim: study/trial evidence about the specific device and body area; for before/after: consent form, dates, unretouched originals; for testimonials: contact details/evidence genuine (CAP).
Q2. Regulatory status declaration by client (agent asks, does not assume): HIS registration number? (Scotland) / CQC? (England) / council licence? / insurance? / who delivers treatment (doctor, nurse, therapist)? / any injectable or prescription product? Block surgical/filler/injectable copy if no registration is evidenced.
Q3. Surgical BBL/liquid BBL pages: named surgeon with GMC number and verifiable register entry; hospital HIS/CQC registration; risk information including mortality context and technique (subcutaneous, ultrasound-guided) per professional advisories; recovery information; alternatives; consent by operating surgeon; reflection period (commonly 2 weeks) and no deposit pressure; complication/revision policy; costs; aftercare and emergency contact; "consultation before any booking" statement [CLINICIAN].
Q4. Non-surgical device pages: contraindications/suitability screening, number of sessions, what results are/aren't expected, patch tests, practitioner qualifications/training and insurance statement, complaints route.
Q5. Data: privacy notice, marketing-consent capture (separate tick-box, unticked), photo consent, withdrawal route, retention.
Q6. Review process: invites to all clients equally, no incentives, disclosure if any incentive.
